## Abstract

AI-based deepfake detectors can achieve very high accuracy on controlled benchmarks, but this does not automatically mean that they are reliable when confronted with new generators, internet-sourced media, compression, or deliberate evasion. This study investigates the reliability of AI-based deepfake detection across image, video, and audio through a structured literature study of ten high-quality publications from 2019-2026. The evidence was analysed in three stages: detection-technique taxonomy, contextual comparison of reported performance, and synthesis of factors that reduce effectiveness. The literature shows that modern detectors use spatial, frequency, temporal, fingerprint, and learned representation approaches for visual media, while audio systems increasingly learn spectro-temporal representations directly from speech. Within-domain results can be near-perfect, yet cross-domain evaluation consistently exposes a generalisation gap. A 2026 cross-modality evaluation reports double-digit performance degradation outside the training distribution and white-box adversarial attack success rates above 80% against undefended models. Compression, unseen manipulation methods, dataset shift, acoustic variation, and adversarial optimisation further reduce reliability. The study concludes that current detectors are conditionally reliable: effective as screening tools under known conditions, but insufficiently robust to be treated as universally dependable evidence of authenticity.

**Keywords:** deepfake detection; generalisation; robustness; adversarial attacks; image forensics; audio anti-spoofing

## 1. Introduction

Deepfakes are synthetic or manipulated images, videos, and audio recordings created with generative models in ways that can make false content appear authentic. Their practical relevance has increased as generation systems have become easier to use and capable of producing increasingly convincing media. The consequences are not limited to entertainment or harmless editing: deepfakes can support impersonation, fraud, misinformation, reputational harm, and the erosion of trust in digital evidence. Recent cross-modality research therefore treats reliable detection as a security and information-integrity problem rather than only a computer-vision benchmark task (Nguyen-Le et al., 2026).

A large research community has developed detectors that look for visual or acoustic traces left by synthesis and manipulation. Controlled benchmarks demonstrate that these systems can perform extremely well. FaceForensics++, for example, established one of the most influential visual benchmarks with more than 1.8 million manipulated images and showed that data-driven detectors can remain strong even when compression is present (Rossler et al., 2019). For audio, AASIST demonstrated that a single graph-attention architecture can detect spoofing artefacts across spectral and temporal intervals, achieving an equal error rate (EER) of 0.83% on the ASVspoof 2019 Logical Access benchmark (Jung et al., 2022). Such results can suggest that the detection problem is close to being solved.

However, benchmark accuracy is only one dimension of reliability. A detector may learn properties that are specific to the dataset, compression pipeline, manipulation method, or generator used during training. When those conditions change, performance can decline sharply. WildDeepfake was created precisely because datasets recorded with a limited number of actors and generated with a small set of tools did not represent the diversity of deepfakes circulating online; its internet-collected material proved substantially more challenging for existing detectors (Zi et al., 2020). More recent work similarly identifies generalisation and robustness, rather than in-domain accuracy alone, as the main barriers to dependable deployment (Nguyen-Le et al., 2026; Ramanaharan et al., 2025).

The objective of this paper is therefore to determine to what extent current AI-based deepfake detection can be considered reliable when evidence from images, videos, and audio is considered together. Reliability is operationalised as a combination of discriminatory performance, generalisation to data or generators outside the training distribution, and robustness to transformations or attacks. Real-world conditions are operationalised as conditions that differ meaningfully from a detector's controlled training benchmark, including internet-sourced media, cross-dataset evaluation, unseen manipulation methods, compression or codecs, diverse recording conditions, and deliberate adversarial evasion.

The main research question is: To what extent can AI-based deepfake detection systems reliably distinguish AI-generated or manipulated media from authentic content? Three subquestions support the analysis: (1) Which AI and machine learning techniques are currently used to detect deepfake images, videos, and audio? (2) How accurately can current deepfake detection systems identify manipulated content under real-world conditions? (3) What factors, such as video compression, new generative models, or deliberate attempts to avoid detection, reduce the effectiveness of deepfake detection systems? The paper first explains the literature-study method, then presents results for the three subquestions, and finally integrates these findings into a conclusion and discussion.

## 2. Method

A structured literature study with document analysis was used. The method was deliberately narrower than a full systematic literature review because the assignment timeframe is four weeks and the research question requires a focused synthesis rather than exhaustive coverage of every deepfake detector. The candidate population consisted of scholarly publications on passive AI-based deepfake detection for image, video, or audio. The final sample contained ten publications selected to provide direct evidence for detector techniques, measured performance, generalisation, robustness, and realistic evaluation conditions.

The source search focused on peer-reviewed conference and journal literature available through publisher and conference repositories including SpringerLink, IEEE/CVF Open Access, NeurIPS Proceedings, ISCA Archive, ScienceDirect, and arXiv where an accessible manuscript was needed. Search terms combined deepfake detection with concepts such as benchmark, generalization/generalisation, cross-dataset, real-world, compression, adversarial attack, audio anti-spoofing, and robustness. The main evidence window was 2020-2026, with FaceForensics++ (2019) retained as a foundational benchmark because it directly addresses compression and remains widely used by later studies.

Publications were included when they directly supported at least one research question and contained a clear evidence basis. Performance studies had to preserve sufficient context to interpret their result, such as dataset, modality, evaluation protocol, or attack condition. Generation-only studies, unsupported commentary, duplicate versions, and papers outside the defined modalities were excluded from the final sample. The sample contains primary empirical studies, benchmark papers, one systematic review, and one recent cross-modality survey that also performs its own empirical evaluation. This combination allows broad field mapping while keeping major reliability claims connected to measured evidence.

A structured extraction matrix was used as the research instrument. For each paper, the analysis recorded publication year, modality, detector or technique family, datasets, evaluation setting, performance metric where relevant, cross-domain or unseen-generator conditions, compression or distortion conditions, adversarial conditions, and stated limitations. Results were then analysed per subquestion. For SQ1, techniques were grouped into a bounded taxonomy. For SQ2, performance was compared contextually rather than pooled across incompatible metrics. For SQ3, evidence was grouped into degradation and failure factors. The main research question was answered by integrating these three analyses and weighting highly generalisable evidence more strongly than isolated in-domain accuracy.

Methodological reliability was supported by stable source selection criteria, explicit operational definitions, traceable extraction fields, and the use of multiple independent studies for important claims. Validity was protected by separating within-domain performance from cross-domain generalisation and by avoiding direct numerical comparison between incompatible visual and audio metrics. The method remains limited by the small purposive sample, the rapid evolution of generative models, and the dominance of face-manipulation research within the visual literature. These limitations are considered explicitly in the discussion.

## 3.1 Detection techniques used across image, video, and audio

The literature shows that current deepfake detection is not based on one universal signal. Instead, detectors exploit different traces depending on the modality and the assumptions of the model. Nguyen-Le et al. (2026) organise visual methods into forensic-based, data-driven, fingerprint-based, and hybrid approaches. Forensic methods search for measurable inconsistencies introduced by generation or manipulation, for example abnormal blending boundaries, local texture differences, frequency artefacts, or inconsistencies in image statistics. Data-driven approaches learn discriminative representations directly from examples, most commonly with convolutional neural networks (CNNs), transformers, or architectures that combine multiple feature domains. Fingerprint-based approaches target recurring traces associated with generation pipelines, while hybrid systems combine learned and explicitly engineered cues.

Benchmark research illustrates how broad the visual detector landscape has become. DeepfakeBench standardised 15 detection methods across nine datasets and showed that model comparison is easily distorted when preprocessing, metrics, or evaluation protocols differ (Yan, Zhang, Yuan, et al., 2023). This is important for SQ1 because technique choice cannot be separated from the way a detector is evaluated. A high-performing CNN under one data pipeline is not directly comparable with a frequency-based or transformer-based detector tested under different conditions.

Several techniques explicitly target generalisation. Self-Blended Images (SBI) creates synthetic training examples from a single pristine face so that the classifier learns generic blending inconsistencies instead of memorising artefacts of one known deepfake method. In cross-dataset evaluation, the approach improved over its baseline by 4.90 percentage points on DFDC and 11.78 percentage points on DFDCP, showing that the training-data construction itself can be a generalisation technique (Shiohara & Yamasaki, 2022). UCF takes a different approach: it disentangles forgery-irrelevant features, method-specific forgery features, and common forgery features, then uses only the common component for detection. The motivation is to reduce overfitting to manipulation-specific patterns that disappear when a new generator is encountered (Yan, Zhang, Fan, et al., 2023).

Video detection can use the same spatial features as image detection, but reliable video analysis may also exploit temporal information. Frame-by-frame image detectors can miss inconsistencies that only become visible over time, such as unstable motion, flickering, or inter-frame coherence violations. The 2026 cross-modality survey therefore treats temporal modelling as an important extension for video rather than assuming that an image detector remains equally reliable when applied independently to frames (Nguyen-Le et al., 2026).

Audio detection uses a related but modality-specific distinction between handcrafted and learned features. Traditional systems rely on spectral or cepstral representations, while newer methods learn directly from waveforms or learned embeddings. AASIST is representative of the latter category: it models spoofing artefacts across spectral and temporal regions using a heterogeneous graph-attention architecture, avoiding the need for a large score-level ensemble (Jung et al., 2022). ASVspoof 5 further demonstrates the current direction of the field by evaluating systems on crowdsourced speech, modern text-to-speech and voice-conversion attacks, neural and conventional codecs, and adversarially optimised attacks (Wang et al., 2024).

Taken together, SQ1 is answered by a layered taxonomy rather than a single dominant model family. Visual detection currently combines spatial, frequency, temporal, fingerprint, and learned representation strategies; audio detection combines spectral-temporal features with increasingly end-to-end learned representations. Across modalities, the common trend is a move away from detecting one known generator toward learning features that remain useful when the generation process changes.

## 3.2 Accuracy under real-world and cross-domain conditions

The strongest pattern in the evidence is the difference between controlled benchmark performance and performance under distribution shift. Within a known benchmark, deepfake detection can be highly accurate. FaceForensics++ established that trained visual detectors could achieve very high accuracy across several manipulation methods and remain effective under compressed conditions relative to earlier forensic baselines (Rossler et al., 2019). AASIST similarly achieved 0.83% EER on the ASVspoof 2019 Logical Access benchmark, outperforming previous anti-spoofing systems in that controlled setting (Jung et al., 2022). These results demonstrate that AI-based detectors are capable of learning strong signals when the evaluation distribution resembles the training distribution.

The picture changes when the detector is tested outside that distribution. Nguyen-Le et al. (2026) independently evaluated methods across ten popular datasets and found a persistent double-digit generalisation loss in out-of-distribution settings, approximately 10-20% depending on protocol and modality. Their analysis distinguishes within-domain accuracy from cross-domain generalisation and argues that near-perfect benchmark results do not establish real-world reliability. This finding is consistent with the 2025 systematic review by Ramanaharan et al., which identifies dataset overfitting as a major cause of weak generalisation and reports that only 46.3% of the selected studies supported generalisation across different deepfake types.

WildDeepfake provides direct evidence of the same problem using internet-sourced video. The dataset contains 7,314 face sequences extracted from 707 deepfake videos collected from the internet rather than generated only in a controlled laboratory pipeline. Baseline detectors that performed strongly on existing datasets suffered substantial performance decreases on WildDeepfake, demonstrating that diversity in identities, scenes, recording pipelines, and unknown generation methods creates a harder test environment (Zi et al., 2020). This makes WildDeepfake particularly relevant to the operational definition of real-world conditions used in this paper.

Generalisation-focused methods improve this situation but do not eliminate it. SBI outperformed a baseline on challenging cross-dataset tests, especially DFDC and DFDCP, by 4.90 and 11.78 percentage points respectively (Shiohara & Yamasaki, 2022). UCF also reports stronger generalisation than contemporary methods by explicitly separating common forgery information from method-specific information (Yan, Zhang, Fan, et al., 2023). These improvements are important, but they still depend on the datasets and protocols used for evaluation. DeepfakeBench shows why standardisation matters: without identical preprocessing, training configuration, datasets, and metrics, apparently superior accuracy can result from experimental differences rather than a genuinely more reliable detector (Yan, Zhang, Yuan, et al., 2023).

Audio evidence reaches a similar conclusion. ASVspoof 5 deliberately increased speaker diversity and acoustic variation, included newer text-to-speech and voice-conversion systems, introduced adversarial attacks, and allowed audio to be affected by conventional and neural codecs. The challenge report states that these attacks significantly compromised baseline systems, although submitted systems achieved substantial improvements (Wang et al., 2024). This demonstrates that a strong anti-spoofing result on an earlier benchmark does not automatically transfer to a newer and more diverse attack environment.

SQ2 can therefore not be answered with one accuracy percentage. Current systems can identify manipulated media with very high accuracy in controlled or in-domain settings, but their performance is materially lower when generators, datasets, capture conditions, or media processing differ from training. Under the real-world definition used here, reliability is best described as variable and context-dependent rather than uniformly high.

## 3.3 Factors that reduce detection effectiveness

Compression and media processing are recurring causes of degraded performance because many detectors depend on subtle artefacts that post-processing can suppress. FaceForensics++ explicitly evaluated multiple compression levels and showed that stronger compression makes detection more difficult, even when learned models remain better than older forensic baselines (Rossler et al., 2019). The same issue appears in audio. ASVspoof 5 includes both conventional codecs and contemporary neural codecs because speech encountered in communication or social-media systems is rarely preserved exactly as generated (Wang et al., 2024). Compression therefore changes the signal distribution and can remove the fine-grained evidence on which a detector relies.

A second factor is the appearance of new or unseen generative models. UCF argues that detectors commonly overfit to forgery-irrelevant characteristics and method-specific patterns, which explains why they fail when the manipulation method changes (Yan, Zhang, Fan, et al., 2023). SBI addresses the same failure mode from the data side by creating more generic fake examples during training (Shiohara & Yamasaki, 2022). The fact that both papers require special mechanisms to improve cross-manipulation performance is itself evidence that standard supervised training tends to learn shortcuts that do not generalise.

A third factor is deliberate evasion. Hou et al. (2023) demonstrate that an attacker can reduce the statistical differences between fake and real images by adversarially applying natural-looking exposure changes, blur, and noise. Their StatAttack and multi-layer MStatAttack were tested against four spatial-domain and two frequency-domain detectors on four datasets and were effective in both white-box and black-box settings. This is especially relevant because the transformations resemble ordinary image degradations rather than conspicuous pixel noise. The broader 2026 evaluation confirms the seriousness of this threat: white-box attacks exceeded 80% attack success against undefended models in the tested robustness setting (Nguyen-Le et al., 2026).

Dataset shift is a fourth factor. WildDeepfake shows that internet-collected examples are harder than curated datasets (Zi et al., 2020), while DeepfakeBench demonstrates that differences in preprocessing and protocol can make performance claims difficult to compare (Yan, Zhang, Yuan, et al., 2023). In audio, ASVspoof 5 extends this shift to speaker diversity, acoustic variation, modern generation technology, codecs, and attacks optimised against surrogate detectors (Wang et al., 2024). These results indicate that 'real-world' degradation is not one condition; it is a combination of shifts in content, acquisition, processing, generation, and attacker behaviour.

Finally, the rapid pace of generation technology reduces the shelf life of detector assumptions. The 2025 systematic review concludes that overfitting and limited dataset diversity remain central barriers to generalisation, and the 2026 survey similarly argues that reliable deployment requires a move from pattern memorisation toward invariant forgery representations (Nguyen-Le et al., 2026; Ramanaharan et al., 2025). SQ3 is therefore answered by five interacting factors: compression and codecs, unseen generators or manipulation methods, adversarial evasion, dataset/acquisition shift, and non-standardised evaluation pipelines. Each can reduce effectiveness independently, and several can occur simultaneously in deployed media.

## 4. Conclusions and Discussion

The main research question asked to what extent AI-based deepfake detection systems can reliably distinguish manipulated or AI-generated media from authentic content. The evidence supports a conditional answer. Current systems can be highly reliable within the distributions and attack types represented by their training and benchmark data, but that reliability does not generalise consistently to real-world conditions. Across image, video, and audio, the largest weakness is not the inability to fit known datasets; it is the loss of performance when the generation method, dataset, compression pipeline, acoustic environment, or attacker behaviour changes.

SQ1 showed that current detection techniques span forensic cues, learned spatial and frequency representations, temporal modelling, generator fingerprints, hybrid models, and audio systems that learn spectro-temporal artefacts. Generalisation-oriented techniques such as SBI and UCF deliberately try to learn common forgery signals rather than manipulation-specific shortcuts. SQ2 showed that controlled benchmarks can produce near-perfect or very low-error results, but cross-domain testing introduces a persistent double-digit performance gap and internet/crowdsourced data are substantially more challenging. SQ3 showed that compression, unseen generators, adversarial attacks, dataset shift, and inconsistent evaluation protocols all reduce effectiveness.

The practical implication is that a detector score should be interpreted as evidence conditioned on an evaluation context, not as a universal probability that content is fake. A model validated only on one benchmark should not be assumed reliable for media from an unknown generator or distribution. For deployment, the findings support four recommendations. First, evaluation should always include cross-dataset or unseen-generator tests in addition to in-domain accuracy. Second, compression, common codecs, resizing, and realistic post-processing should be part of robustness testing. Third, adversarial evaluation should be treated as a standard security requirement for high-impact applications. Fourth, reported performance should preserve dataset, metric, preprocessing, and attack context so that results can be compared transparently. In high-stakes use, detector output should therefore support a wider verification process rather than act as the sole basis for an authenticity decision.

The reliability of this literature study is strengthened by explicit selection rules, a stable extraction structure, and triangulation across benchmark papers, primary empirical studies, a systematic review, and a recent cross-modality empirical survey. Nevertheless, the review is not exhaustive. Ten publications were purposively selected for direct relevance, which creates a risk of missing contradictory evidence. The visual literature also concentrates heavily on face manipulation, while deepfake images can now be generated without face swapping. Metrics differ across modalities: visual studies frequently report accuracy or AUC, while audio studies often use EER and task-specific cost functions. This heterogeneity makes a pooled meta-analysis inappropriate.

Validity is further limited by the operational definition of real-world conditions. Internet-sourced datasets, cross-domain tests, codecs, and adversarial attacks approximate deployment challenges, but no single benchmark reproduces all real-world situations. New generators can also appear faster than peer-reviewed evaluation cycles. The results therefore justify a bounded conclusion about current passive detection rather than a permanent judgement about future systems.

In terms of usability, the findings are directly useful for selecting and evaluating deepfake detectors: benchmark accuracy alone is insufficient, and generalisation and robustness tests are essential. The evidence does not justify a universal threshold or a single 'best' detector across image, video, and audio. Future work should prioritise diverse and continuously updated datasets, standardised cross-domain protocols, robust feature learning, and evaluation against realistic adaptive attacks. On the evidence reviewed here, AI-based deepfake detection is an effective but fallible layer of defence: strong under known conditions, materially less dependable when those conditions change.
