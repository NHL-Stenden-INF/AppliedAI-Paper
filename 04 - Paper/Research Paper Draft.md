## Abstract

AI-based deepfake detectors can achieve very high accuracy on controlled benchmarks, but this does not automatically mean that they are reliable when confronted with new generators, internet-sourced media, compression, or deliberate evasion. This study investigates the reliability of AI-based deepfake detection across image, video, and audio through a structured literature study of ten selected scholarly publications from 2019-2026. The evidence was analysed in three stages: detection-technique taxonomy, contextual comparison of reported performance, and synthesis of factors that reduce effectiveness. The literature shows that modern detectors use spatial, frequency, temporal, fingerprint, and learned representation approaches for visual media, while audio systems increasingly learn spectro-temporal representations directly from speech. Within-domain results can be near-perfect, yet cross-domain evaluation consistently exposes a generalisation gap. A 2026 cross-modality evaluation reports double-digit performance degradation outside the training distribution and white-box adversarial attack success rates above 80% against undefended models. Compression, unseen manipulation methods, dataset shift, acoustic variation, and adversarial optimisation further reduce reliability. The study concludes that current detectors are conditionally reliable: effective as screening tools under known conditions, but insufficiently robust to be treated as universally dependable evidence of authenticity.

**Keywords:** deepfake detection; generalisation; robustness; adversarial attacks; image forensics; audio anti-spoofing

## 1. Introduction

Deepfakes are synthetic or manipulated images, videos, and audio recordings created with generative models in ways that can make false content appear authentic. Their practical relevance has increased as generation systems have become easier to use and capable of producing increasingly convincing media. The consequences are not limited to entertainment or harmless editing: deepfakes can support impersonation, fraud, misinformation, reputational harm, and the erosion of trust in digital evidence. Recent cross-modality research therefore treats reliable detection as a security and information-integrity problem rather than only a computer-vision benchmark task (Nguyen-Le et al., 2026).

A large research community has developed detectors that look for visual or acoustic traces left by synthesis and manipulation. Controlled benchmarks demonstrate that these systems can perform extremely well. FaceForensics++, for example, established one of the most influential visual benchmarks with more than 1.8 million manipulated images and showed that data-driven detectors can remain strong even when compression is present (Rössler et al., 2019). For audio, AASIST demonstrated that a single graph-attention architecture can detect spoofing artefacts across spectral and temporal intervals, achieving an equal error rate (EER) of 0.83% on the ASVspoof 2019 Logical Access benchmark (Jung et al., 2022). Such results can suggest that the detection problem is close to being solved.

However, benchmark accuracy is only one dimension of reliability. A detector may learn properties that are specific to the dataset, compression pipeline, manipulation method, or generator used during training. When those conditions change, performance can decline sharply. WildDeepfake was created precisely because datasets recorded with a limited number of actors and generated with a small set of tools did not represent the diversity of deepfakes circulating online; its internet-collected material proved substantially more challenging for existing detectors (Zi et al., 2020). More recent work similarly identifies generalisation and robustness, rather than in-domain accuracy alone, as the main barriers to dependable deployment (Nguyen-Le et al., 2026; Ramanaharan et al., 2025).

The objective of this paper is therefore to determine to what extent current AI-based deepfake detection can be considered reliable when evidence from images, videos, and audio is considered together. Reliability is operationalised as a combination of discriminatory performance, generalisation to data or generators outside the training distribution, and robustness to transformations or attacks. Real-world conditions are operationalised as conditions that differ meaningfully from a detector's controlled training benchmark, including internet-sourced media, cross-dataset evaluation, unseen manipulation methods, compression or codecs, diverse recording conditions, and deliberate adversarial evasion. The scope is limited to passive AI/ML-based detection of image, video, and audio content; provenance or watermarking systems, legal analysis, and the development of a new detector are outside the scope.

The main research question is: To what extent can AI-based deepfake detection systems reliably distinguish AI-generated or manipulated media from authentic content? Three subquestions support the analysis: (1) Which AI and machine learning techniques are currently used to detect deepfake images, videos, and audio? (2) How accurately can current deepfake detection systems identify manipulated content under real-world conditions? (3) What factors, such as video compression, new generative models, or deliberate attempts to avoid detection, reduce the effectiveness of deepfake detection systems? The paper first explains the literature-study method, then presents results for the three subquestions, and finally integrates these findings into a conclusion and discussion.

## 2. Method

A structured literature study with document analysis was used. The method was deliberately narrower than a full systematic literature review because the assignment timeframe is four weeks and the research question requires a focused synthesis rather than exhaustive coverage of every deepfake detector. The candidate population consisted of scholarly publications on passive AI-based deepfake detection for image, video, or audio. The final sample contained ten publications selected to provide direct evidence for detector techniques, measured performance, generalisation, robustness, and realistic evaluation conditions.

The source search focused on peer-reviewed conference and journal literature available through publisher and conference repositories including SpringerLink, IEEE/CVF Open Access, NeurIPS Proceedings, ISCA Archive, and ScienceDirect. Searches and source verification were conducted in September 2026. Search terms combined deepfake detection with concepts such as benchmark, generalization/generalisation, cross-dataset, real-world, compression, adversarial attack, audio anti-spoofing, and robustness. Publisher or conference versions were treated as the primary records; arXiv copies were consulted only when they mirrored an included peer-reviewed publication. The main evidence window was 2020-2026, with FaceForensics++ (2019) retained as a foundational benchmark because it directly addresses compression and remains widely used by later studies.

Publications were included when they directly supported at least one research question and contained a clear evidence basis. Performance studies had to preserve sufficient context to interpret their result, such as dataset, modality, evaluation protocol, or attack condition. Generation-only studies, unsupported commentary, duplicate versions, and papers outside the defined modalities were excluded from the final sample. The sample contains primary empirical studies, benchmark papers, one systematic review, and one recent cross-modality survey that also performs its own empirical evaluation. This combination allows broad field mapping while keeping major reliability claims connected to measured evidence.

A structured extraction matrix was used as the research instrument. For each paper, the analysis recorded publication year, modality, detector or technique family, datasets, evaluation setting, performance metric where relevant, cross-domain or unseen-generator conditions, compression or distortion conditions, adversarial conditions, and stated limitations. Results were then analysed per subquestion. For SQ1, techniques were grouped into a bounded taxonomy. For SQ2, performance was compared contextually rather than pooled across incompatible metrics. For SQ3, evidence was grouped into degradation and failure factors. The main research question was answered by integrating these three analyses and weighting highly generalisable evidence more strongly than isolated in-domain accuracy.

Methodological reliability was supported by stable source selection criteria, explicit operational definitions, traceable extraction fields, and the use of multiple independent studies for important claims. Validity was protected by separating within-domain performance from cross-domain generalisation and by avoiding direct numerical comparison between incompatible visual and audio metrics. The method remains limited by the small purposive sample, the rapid evolution of generative models, and the dominance of face-manipulation research within the visual literature. These limitations are considered explicitly in the discussion.

### 3.1 Detection techniques used across image, video, and audio

The reviewed literature shows that deepfake detection is not a single technical task with one dominant solution. Detectors operate on different media modalities, target different manipulation processes, and learn different indicators of authenticity. Nguyen-Le et al. (2026) group visual approaches into forensic-based, data-driven, fingerprint-based, and hybrid methods. DeepfakeBench reaches a similar practical conclusion from a benchmarking perspective: its fifteen implemented methods span multiple detector families and cannot be understood meaningfully without also considering preprocessing, training strategy, backbone choice, dataset composition, and evaluation protocol (Yan, Zhang, Yuan, et al., 2023). For the present research question, the important distinction is therefore not simply which architecture obtains the highest score, but which type of evidence a detector uses and how dependent that evidence is on the environment in which the model was trained.

#### 3.1.1 Spatial and forensic visual cues

A large group of image and video detectors searches for spatial artefacts in individual frames. These artefacts can arise because generated or manipulated regions do not perfectly reproduce the statistics of authentic camera imagery or because a synthetic region must be blended into surrounding content. Examples include local texture differences, blending boundaries, inconsistent colour or illumination, abnormal high-frequency details, and other statistical irregularities. FaceForensics++ was influential because it provided a large standardised environment in which data-driven detectors could learn these cues across four manipulation families: DeepFakes, Face2Face, FaceSwap, and NeuralTextures (Rössler et al., 2019). The dataset contains more than 1.8 million manipulated images and explicitly includes compression, making it useful for studying how detector cues survive media processing.

The strength of spatial detectors is that they can learn subtle patterns that are difficult for human observers to identify consistently. Their weakness is that the patterns may be specific to the manipulation pipeline or dataset. A model can therefore obtain a high in-domain score while partly relying on properties that are incidental to that benchmark rather than properties shared by deepfakes in general. The UCF study describes this as overfitting to forgery-irrelevant features and method-specific forgery features (Yan, Zhang, Fan, et al., 2023). This distinction is central to reliability: if a model uses a feature that disappears when the generator changes, a high benchmark score demonstrates classification ability under known conditions but provides limited evidence about unseen content.

#### 3.1.2 Frequency-domain and generator-related cues

Frequency-domain detectors examine patterns that may be less obvious in the spatial image but become visible after transforming the signal. Generative and image-processing pipelines can leave characteristic spectral distributions because synthesis, upsampling, filtering, and compression alter how energy is distributed across frequencies. Nguyen-Le et al. (2026) include frequency-oriented methods within the broader landscape of forensic and learned detection approaches, while Hou et al. (2023) explicitly distinguish spatial-domain and frequency-domain detectors when evaluating adversarial evasion. Their attack was tested on four spatial-based and two frequency-based detectors, indicating that both families are sufficiently established to require separate robustness evaluation.

Fingerprint-based methods are related but conceptually distinct. Instead of detecting a generic inconsistency between real and fake media, they attempt to exploit recurring traces associated with a generation process. Such traces can be highly discriminative when the generator is known. However, they also illustrate the core reliability problem examined in this paper: a generator-specific fingerprint can become less useful when a new architecture, post-processing pipeline, or manipulation method produces a different statistical signature. The 2026 survey therefore treats robustness and out-of-distribution generalisation as separate requirements from ordinary within-dataset discrimination (Nguyen-Le et al., 2026).

#### 3.1.3 Generalisation-oriented visual techniques

Two reviewed methods directly address the risk of learning manipulation-specific shortcuts. Self-Blended Images (SBI) changes the training-data construction rather than only the detector architecture. It creates synthetic fake examples by blending pseudo source and target regions derived from one pristine image. Because the training examples are not produced by one fixed external deepfake generator, the classifier is encouraged to learn more generic blending and statistical inconsistencies (Shiohara & Yamasaki, 2022). The method was evaluated on multiple datasets under cross-dataset and cross-manipulation protocols. On DFDC and DFDCP, where the domain gap is particularly challenging, SBI improved its baseline by 4.90 and 11.78 percentage points respectively in the reported cross-dataset evaluation. These results show that the design of training data can itself be a reliability intervention.

UCF approaches the same problem at the representation level. It decomposes information into forgery-irrelevant features, method-specific forgery features, and common forgery features. Multi-task learning and contrastive regularisation are used to separate these components, after which only the common forgery representation is used for generalisable detection (Yan, Zhang, Fan, et al., 2023). The importance of this design is not only its reported improvement over contemporary methods. It provides a conceptual explanation for why ordinary detectors can fail: a model may correctly classify known manipulations for the wrong reason, because it has learned method-specific evidence rather than a representation that survives changes in the generation process.

SBI and UCF therefore represent two complementary routes to generalisation. SBI changes the examples from which the detector learns; UCF changes which part of the learned representation is retained for classification. Both methods support the same broader finding: reliability requires explicitly reducing dependence on dataset- or method-specific artefacts. Generalisation does not automatically emerge from high-capacity supervised learning, even when in-domain accuracy is high.

#### 3.1.4 Temporal information in video

Video adds an additional evidence source that is absent from single images: time. A frame-level detector can identify spatial inconsistencies independently in each frame, but a video also contains motion, temporal coherence, and changes in appearance across consecutive frames. Nguyen-Le et al. (2026) treat temporal modelling as an important category because manipulations can introduce instability that is weak or invisible in one frame but clearer over a sequence. Examples include inconsistent facial details across frames, irregular motion patterns, or temporal changes in generated regions that do not match the surrounding video.

Temporal evidence is potentially valuable because it gives the detector more than one opportunity to observe a manipulation. At the same time, it introduces practical dependencies. Frame rate, video compression, scene cuts, motion blur, resolution, and the number of usable frames can all affect the available temporal signal. A reliable video detector therefore cannot be evaluated only as an image classifier applied repeatedly. The modality itself creates both additional information and additional sources of distribution shift.

#### 3.1.5 Audio anti-spoofing techniques

Audio deepfake detection uses a different signal space but shows the same transition from handcrafted artefacts toward learned representations. Traditional countermeasures can use spectral or cepstral features that characterise the acoustic signal, while newer architectures learn directly from waveforms or learned feature representations. AASIST is a representative example: it models spoofing artefacts across spectral and temporal intervals using graph-attention mechanisms, integrating information that earlier systems often handled through multiple separate subsystems (Jung et al., 2022). On ASVspoof 2019 Logical Access, AASIST reports an EER of 0.83%, demonstrating that learned spectro-temporal features can be extremely discriminative under the benchmark conditions for which the system was evaluated.

ASVspoof 5 shows how the audio field has moved beyond testing only one fixed set of synthetic-speech attacks. The 2024 challenge uses crowdsourced speech from a much larger and more varied speaker population, modern text-to-speech and voice-conversion attacks, adversarial attacks, and both conventional and neural codecs (Wang et al., 2024). This evolution is technically significant. It means that a detector is increasingly judged not only on its ability to recognise known synthesis artefacts, but on whether those representations remain useful after changes in speaker, acoustic environment, codec, or attack strategy.

#### 3.1.6 Technique taxonomy and reliability implications

Table 1 summarises the detector families identified in the reviewed evidence and the principal reliability risk associated with each family. The table is not intended as an exhaustive catalogue of all deepfake detectors. Instead, it links the technique landscape directly to the main research question by showing what each family depends on.

**Table 1. Detector technique families and reliability implications**

| Technique family | Main signal or strategy | Modalities in reviewed evidence | Main reliability risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spatial / learned visual detection | Local textures, blending artefacts, learned frame-level representations | Image, video | Can learn dataset- or manipulation-specific shortcuts |
| Frequency-domain detection | Spectral/statistical differences between real and generated imagery | Image, video | Post-processing or new generators can change the frequency signature |
| Fingerprint / generator-trace detection | Recurring traces associated with synthesis pipelines | Image, video | Strong dependence on generator identity or generation process |

| Spectro-temporal anti-spoofing | Learned spectral and temporal speech artefacts, e.g. AASIST | Audio | Sensitive to new TTS/VC systems, speakers, acoustics, and codecs |

The answer to SQ1 is therefore that current systems use multiple complementary AI and machine-learning strategies rather than one universal detector. More importantly, the technique review reveals a recurring trade-off. Highly specific artefacts can support very strong discrimination when test conditions match training, whereas more general representations are deliberately designed to sacrifice dependence on one generator in favour of broader transfer. This trade-off becomes visible in the performance evidence considered for SQ2.

### 3.2 Accuracy under real-world and cross-domain conditions

A direct answer to SQ2 requires separating three questions that are often collapsed into one accuracy number: how well a detector performs on a known benchmark, how well it transfers to a different dataset or manipulation process, and how well it survives media transformations or attacks that occur after generation. The reviewed studies use accuracy, AUC, EER, and challenge-specific metrics, so a pooled percentage would be misleading. DeepfakeBench specifically identifies non-standardised preprocessing, experimental settings, evaluation strategies, and metrics as causes of unfair or misleading comparison between detectors (Yan, Zhang, Yuan, et al., 2023). Consequently, the results below preserve the evaluation context instead of ranking all systems on one scale.

#### 3.2.1 Strong performance under controlled conditions

Controlled benchmarks establish that deepfake detection is technically feasible and can be highly effective. FaceForensics++ demonstrated that data-driven visual detectors could distinguish multiple manipulation types at high accuracy and outperform human observers, even when compression was introduced (Rössler et al., 2019). The importance of this result is that it rules out the idea that synthetic media are inherently undetectable. When the manipulation families are represented in training and the data pipeline is sufficiently controlled, discriminative artefacts exist and machine-learning models can exploit them.

The audio evidence is similarly strong within a defined benchmark. AASIST reports an EER of 0.83% on the ASVspoof 2019 Logical Access evaluation set (Jung et al., 2022). EER is the operating point at which false-accept and false-reject error rates are equal, so a lower value indicates better separation between bona fide and spoofed speech. The result demonstrates that a modern anti-spoofing architecture can separate known classes of synthetic and converted speech with very low error under the benchmark conditions.

These in-domain results are necessary evidence of detector capability, but they are not sufficient evidence of general reliability. A benchmark can only test the generators, data characteristics, preprocessing choices, and attack types represented in that benchmark. The central empirical question is therefore what happens when one or more of those conditions change.

#### 3.2.2 Cross-dataset generalisation gap

The clearest recurring pattern in the reviewed literature is a performance gap between in-domain and out-of-distribution evaluation. Nguyen-Le et al. (2026), in a recent cross-modality survey with empirical evaluation, report persistent double-digit degradation in out-of-distribution testing, with approximately 10-15% performance loss in the OOD scenarios summarised by the study. Their main contribution to the present paper is not a single detector ranking but the finding that generalisation remains a cross-modality problem affecting image, video, and audio systems.

The systematic review by Ramanaharan et al. (2025) independently reaches a similar conclusion for video detection. It identifies overfitting, limited dataset diversity, and insufficient evaluation on unseen manipulation types as recurring weaknesses in the field. The review reports that only 46.3% of the selected studies supported generalisation across different deepfake types. This does not mean that the remaining studies all failed under exactly the same protocol; rather, it shows that evidence for broad generalisation is substantially less common than evidence for good performance on the datasets used during development.

UCF provides a more granular example of this difficulty. In the cross-dataset table reported in the paper, a conventional Xception baseline obtains an average AUC of 0.702 across the listed combinations of training conditions and external datasets, while other generalisation-focused methods improve on that baseline (Yan, Zhang, Fan, et al., 2023). The exact values depend on which manipulation types are held out and which target dataset is used, but the table illustrates a broader point: when the evaluation distribution changes, performance is no longer near the upper limit suggested by familiar in-domain benchmarks. The generalisation problem is therefore observable even before introducing adversarial attacks.

#### 3.2.3 Internet-sourced deepfakes as a real-world proxy

WildDeepfake makes the generalisation problem more concrete by changing where the fake videos come from. Instead of generating all manipulations inside one controlled research pipeline, the dataset contains 7,314 face sequences extracted from 707 deepfake videos collected from the internet (Zi et al., 2020). This changes several variables at once: identities, scenes, generation tools, video processing, editing history, resolution, and the unknown choices made by creators before uploading content.

Existing baseline detectors show substantial performance reductions on WildDeepfake compared with controlled datasets (Zi et al., 2020). The significance of this finding is methodological as well as technical. Internet-collected data are not a perfect model of every deployment environment, but they represent a stronger test of external validity because the detector cannot rely on a single research team's generation and preprocessing pipeline. WildDeepfake therefore supports the operational definition of real-world conditions used in this paper: a condition becomes more realistic when the detector faces sources and transformations that were not tightly controlled during model development.

This evidence also explains why high laboratory accuracy can coexist with practical uncertainty. A detector can genuinely be excellent at distinguishing the fake examples present in its benchmark while still being unreliable for content produced by different tools or processed through different online platforms. The two statements are not contradictory; they refer to different distributions.

#### 3.2.4 Improvements from generalisation-focused methods

The fact that cross-domain performance is weaker does not mean that progress is absent. SBI shows that altering training-data generation can improve performance on external datasets. It exceeds its baseline by 4.90 percentage points on DFDC and 11.78 percentage points on DFDCP in the reported cross-dataset evaluation (Shiohara & Yamasaki, 2022). These gains are important because they occur where existing methods suffer from domain gap rather than only on the dataset used to create the training examples.

UCF likewise reports superior generalisation to contemporary methods by isolating common forgery features (Yan, Zhang, Fan, et al., 2023). Taken together, SBI and UCF demonstrate that the generalisation gap is not fixed: detector design can reduce it. However, neither paper supports the stronger claim that the gap has been eliminated. Their methods are still evaluated on a finite set of datasets and manipulation families. A future generator may differ from all of them. The correct interpretation is therefore that generalisation can be improved through targeted design, while remaining a separate property that must be measured explicitly.

#### 3.2.5 Compression as evidence that accuracy is condition-dependent

FaceForensics++ directly illustrates how the same underlying content becomes harder to classify when media quality changes. The benchmark evaluates raw and compressed material and reports that stronger compression reduces the information available to the detector (Rössler et al., 2019). Its supplementary manipulation-classification results, for example, decrease from 99.03% on raw data to 95.42% under high-quality compression and 80.49% under low-quality compression. These values concern classification of the manipulation method rather than the binary deepfake-detection score, so they are not used here as a direct detector accuracy comparison. They nevertheless provide quantitative evidence that compression alters the forensic signal on which deepfake analysis depends.

This distinction matters for real-world use because media are routinely recompressed when uploaded, forwarded, edited, or stored. A detector validated only on high-quality source files may therefore face a different evidence distribution when analysing social-media video or messaging-app content. Compression is not an adversarial attack in the ordinary case; it is a normal part of media handling. Robustness to it is consequently part of baseline deployment reliability rather than an optional security feature.

#### 3.2.6 Audio performance under broader challenge conditions

The contrast between AASIST and ASVspoof 5 shows the same benchmark-to-deployment transition in audio. AASIST's 0.83% EER on ASVspoof 2019 LA demonstrates strong separation under a defined set of logical-access spoofing attacks (Jung et al., 2022). ASVspoof 5 deliberately increases the diversity of the problem by using crowdsourced speech from many more speakers and acoustic conditions, modern text-to-speech and voice-conversion systems, codecs, and adversarial attacks (Wang et al., 2024). The challenge paper reports that these attacks significantly compromise baseline systems, although submitted systems obtain substantial improvements.

The audio evidence is especially useful because it prevents the reliability analysis from becoming a purely face-manipulation argument. The underlying pattern is modality-independent: models can learn highly discriminative artefacts on known data, but performance must be reassessed when the generator, recording environment, speaker distribution, or transmission channel changes. For audio, codecs and acoustic conditions play a role analogous to image/video compression and acquisition shift.

SQ2 is therefore answered conditionally. Current detectors can be extremely accurate under controlled and familiar conditions, but there is no defensible universal accuracy value for real-world media. The strongest evidence across the ten sources is not that all systems fail, but that the variance between in-domain and shifted conditions is itself a defining property of current detector reliability.

### 3.3 Factors that reduce detection effectiveness

SQ3 asks why effectiveness falls. The reviewed evidence supports five main factor classes: compression and codecs, unseen generators or manipulation methods, deliberate evasion, dataset/acquisition shift, and evaluation-pipeline differences. These factors are analytically separable, but in actual media they can occur together. A deepfake from a new generator may also be compressed, uploaded through an unknown platform, recorded or re-recorded, and intentionally modified to evade a detector.

#### 3.3.1 Compression, codecs, and ordinary post-processing

Compression changes the signal without changing the semantic content of the media. For deepfake detection this is important because many detectors rely on low-level artefacts that are weaker than the main visual or acoustic content. FaceForensics++ demonstrates that increasing visual compression reduces the quality of manipulation evidence and makes forensic analysis harder (Rössler et al., 2019). The decrease in the supplementary manipulation-classification figures from raw to strongly compressed video illustrates the magnitude with which processing can alter measurable cues, even before considering a new generator.

ASVspoof 5 treats the same issue explicitly for audio by incorporating conventional and neural codecs (Wang et al., 2024). Codec robustness is relevant because synthetic speech may be generated at high quality but later transmitted through telephony, messaging, streaming, or platform-specific compression. A detector trained on clean waveform artefacts may therefore receive a transformed signal at inference time. Across modalities, compression is a distribution shift created by normal media handling rather than by generation itself.

The evidence suggests two reliability consequences. First, a detector should not be described as robust merely because it tolerates mild compression inside its own benchmark; the strength and type of transformation matter. Second, the same preprocessing can affect detector families differently. A spatial detector, frequency detector, and temporal detector do not necessarily lose the same information when a video is recompressed. Robustness is therefore model- and transformation-specific.

#### 3.3.2 New or unseen generators and manipulation methods

Unseen generation methods are the most direct challenge to supervised deepfake detection. Training labels tell a model which examples are real and fake, but they do not force the model to learn the causal property that all future fakes will share. If a detector instead learns an artefact associated with one generator, that artefact may disappear when another generator is used. UCF's separation of method-specific and common forgery features is designed precisely around this problem (Yan, Zhang, Fan, et al., 2023).

SBI addresses the same weakness by removing the dependence on a fixed external forgery generator when creating part of the training data (Shiohara & Yamasaki, 2022). The reported cross-dataset improvements indicate that more generic training artefacts can increase transfer. Ramanaharan et al. (2025), however, show at review level that generalisation remains inconsistent across the field. The implication is that unseen-generator performance is not a solved extension of ordinary classification; it is a distinct evaluation target that requires specific training and testing choices.

This factor also reduces the useful lifetime of a detector. A model can be state of the art at publication time yet face new generation families later. Nguyen-Le et al. (2026) identify the continuing pace of generative development as a reason to favour invariant or transferable forensic representations over memorised artefacts. Reliability is therefore partly temporal: it depends on whether the detector remains valid as the generation ecosystem changes.

#### 3.3.3 Deliberate adversarial evasion

Deliberate evasion creates a stronger threat model because the media are modified specifically to reduce detector confidence. Hou et al. (2023) propose StatAttack and MStatAttack, which use exposure changes, blur, and noise in an adversarial optimisation process. Instead of adding visually conspicuous random perturbations, the attack selects natural-looking degradations that reduce statistical differences between fake and real images. The experiments cover four spatial-domain and two frequency-domain detectors across four datasets and demonstrate effectiveness in both white-box and black-box settings.

This result is significant for two reasons. First, it shows that an attacker does not need to reproduce the internal feature representation of every detector exactly; transfer can occur across models. Second, the attack manipulations resemble transformations that might already occur during ordinary media processing. This makes it harder to separate 'benign degradation' from 'malicious evasion' by visual inspection alone.

Nguyen-Le et al. (2026) extend the robustness concern across modalities and report white-box attack success rates above 80% against undefended models in the tested setting. ASVspoof 5 incorporates adversarial attacks into the speech-deepfake challenge for the first time and similarly reports that attacks significantly compromise baseline systems (Wang et al., 2024). Together, these sources show that adversarial robustness cannot be inferred from ordinary clean-test performance. A detector can be highly accurate on untouched benchmark samples and still be vulnerable when an attacker is allowed to optimise the input.

#### 3.3.4 Dataset, identity, acquisition, and environment shift

Dataset shift is broader than unseen generation method. It includes differences in people, scenes, cameras, microphones, resolution, recording environments, background noise, editing, and other properties that may correlate with the label in a research dataset. WildDeepfake changes many of these variables simultaneously by using internet-sourced videos (Zi et al., 2020). The substantial reduction in baseline performance indicates that detectors can depend on dataset-specific regularities even when they were designed to learn manipulation artefacts.

In audio, ASVspoof 5 expands speaker and acoustic diversity through crowdsourcing (Wang et al., 2024). This is the acoustic equivalent of moving from curated face datasets toward less controlled real-world material. The shared lesson is that data diversity matters not merely because it increases training-set size, but because it reduces the chance that the model confuses environmental regularities with evidence of authenticity.

Dataset shift also complicates interpretation of model comparisons. If one method is evaluated on a clean, balanced dataset and another on a more heterogeneous internet dataset, their headline scores do not measure the same difficulty. DeepfakeBench's standardisation effort is therefore relevant not only for reproducibility but also for reliability claims: without a shared evaluation pipeline, performance differences can reflect the benchmark rather than the detector (Yan, Zhang, Yuan, et al., 2023).

#### 3.3.5 Non-standardised preprocessing and evaluation

DeepfakeBench identifies inconsistent data processing, experimental settings, metrics, and evaluation strategies as a major problem in the literature (Yan, Zhang, Yuan, et al., 2023). This factor differs from the others because it does not necessarily reduce the true technical capability of a detector. Instead, it can reduce the reliability of the *evidence used to judge* that capability. Two methods may appear to differ because they used different face crops, augmentation, train/test splits, backbone settings, or metrics.

For the present paper this finding justifies the decision not to pool reported percentages. A detector that reports 99% accuracy on one balanced in-domain dataset cannot be declared more reliable than a detector that reports a lower AUC on an unseen internet dataset. The evaluation questions are different. Reliability claims therefore require transparent reporting of training data, test data, metric, preprocessing, and whether the manipulation or generator was seen during training.

The systematic review by Ramanaharan et al. (2025) supports the same concern from a broader perspective: generalisation claims are difficult to compare when studies define cross-dataset or cross-manipulation evaluation differently. Standardised benchmarking is consequently part of the solution, but it must include difficult shifted conditions rather than standardise only easy in-domain tests.

#### 3.3.6 Interaction between failure factors

The five factors are most dangerous when they interact. Consider a video generated by a method absent from the detector's training set, recompressed by a social platform, and intentionally blurred before upload. The detector then faces generator shift, codec shift, acquisition/post-processing shift, and potentially adversarial manipulation at the same time. None of the reviewed benchmarks fully represents every such combination. This explains why 'real-world reliability' cannot be reduced to one extra test dataset.

SQ3 is therefore answered by a set of interacting boundary conditions rather than one isolated weakness. Compression can erase cues, unseen generators can invalidate learned shortcuts, adversarial attacks can deliberately exploit model sensitivity, dataset shift can introduce unfamiliar content and environments, and non-standardised evaluation can make performance look more transferable than it is. These factors explain why detector reliability decreases as the evaluation moves away from the controlled conditions in which a model was developed.

### 3.4 Integrated reliability profile across the three subquestions

The three subquestions together reveal a consistent structure. SQ1 shows that detectors learn a wide range of spatial, frequency, temporal, fingerprint, and spectro-temporal signals. SQ2 shows that these signals can be highly discriminative when the test distribution resembles training, but that performance decreases under cross-domain and more realistic conditions. SQ3 explains the mechanism behind that decrease: the signal itself may be weakened by processing, absent in a new generator, confounded by a new dataset, or intentionally manipulated by an attacker.

This integration is important because it prevents two opposite overstatements. The first overstatement would be that deepfake detection is unreliable because some detectors fail under distribution shift. That conclusion would ignore the strong in-domain results from FaceForensics++, AASIST, and other benchmark studies. The second overstatement would be that deepfake detection is reliable because individual models achieve very high benchmark scores. That conclusion would ignore WildDeepfake, cross-dataset evaluations, the systematic review of generalisation, and adversarial evidence. The full evidence base supports a more bounded interpretation: current detectors are powerful classifiers whose dependability is conditional on how closely deployment conditions match the evidence used to validate them.

The strongest recurring property is therefore not one architecture but *evaluation breadth*. Methods such as SBI and UCF are notable because they are designed and evaluated with transfer in mind, while DeepfakeBench is important because it makes evaluation conditions more transparent. WildDeepfake and ASVspoof 5 are important because they make the test distribution harder and more diverse. Hou et al. (2023) are important because they add an adaptive attacker. Nguyen-Le et al. (2026) integrate these concerns across image, video, and audio. Together, the literature moves the meaning of reliability away from a single clean-test score toward evidence across multiple stages of the reliability chain shown in Figure 1.

A practical implication can be derived without moving into recommendations yet: the confidence that can reasonably be placed in a detector result depends on the match between the current input and the conditions under which the detector was validated. If the media type, generator, quality, processing pipeline, and attack model are close to the validation environment, the benchmark evidence is more directly applicable. As those conditions become less similar, uncertainty increases unless the detector has been tested specifically for the relevant shift. This relationship between validation coverage and confidence is the central empirical result connecting SQ1-SQ3 to the main research question.

Finally, the evidence shows that image, video, and audio differ in their signals but not in the structure of the reliability problem. Visual detectors can overfit to manipulation artefacts; audio detectors can overfit to synthesis and channel conditions. Video adds temporal information but also additional processing variation. Across all three modalities, high capacity improves the ability to fit known examples, while generalisation requires representative data, transferable features, robustness testing, and transparent evaluation. This cross-modality consistency strengthens the conclusion that reliability should be judged as a combination of discrimination, generalisation, and robustness rather than accuracy alone.

A final pattern concerns the strength of the available evidence itself. Evidence is strongest for the proposition that detectors can discriminate known real and fake examples under controlled conditions, because this is supported by mature benchmarks such as FaceForensics++ and ASVspoof. Evidence for cross-domain transfer is also substantial, but it is more heterogeneous because studies use different target datasets, held-out manipulations, and metrics. Robustness evidence is narrower again: compression, codecs, and selected adversarial attacks have been studied directly, but the space of possible transformations and adaptive attacks is much larger than any benchmark can cover. This creates an evidence gradient from well-characterised benchmark capability toward more uncertain deployment reliability. DeepfakeBench addresses the first part of this problem by standardising evaluation, while WildDeepfake, ASVspoof 5, UCF, SBI, and the adversarial studies progressively widen the conditions under which reliability is tested. The 2026 cross-modality evaluation is valuable because it connects these separate lines of evidence, but it does not remove the need for modality- and threat-specific validation. Consequently, the strongest result of the literature study is not that one detector family is universally superior. It is that confidence in a detector should increase only when validation covers the same kinds of distribution shift, processing, and attacks that are expected in the intended use environment.

## 4. Conclusions and Discussion

The main research question asked to what extent AI-based deepfake detection systems can reliably distinguish manipulated or AI-generated media from authentic content. The evidence supports a conditional answer. Current systems can be highly reliable within the distributions and attack types represented by their training and benchmark data, but that reliability does not generalise consistently to real-world conditions. Across image, video, and audio, the largest weakness is not the inability to fit known datasets; it is the loss of performance when the generation method, dataset, compression pipeline, acoustic environment, or attacker behaviour changes.

SQ1 showed that current detection techniques span forensic cues, learned spatial and frequency representations, temporal modelling, generator fingerprints, hybrid models, and audio systems that learn spectro-temporal artefacts. Generalisation-oriented techniques such as SBI and UCF deliberately try to learn common forgery signals rather than manipulation-specific shortcuts. SQ2 showed that controlled benchmarks can produce near-perfect or very low-error results, but cross-domain testing introduces a persistent double-digit performance gap and internet/crowdsourced data are substantially more challenging. SQ3 showed that compression, unseen generators, adversarial attacks, dataset shift, and inconsistent evaluation protocols all reduce effectiveness.

The practical implication is that a detector score should be interpreted as evidence conditioned on an evaluation context, not as a universal probability that content is fake. A model validated only on one benchmark should not be assumed reliable for media from an unknown generator or distribution. For deployment, the findings support four recommendations. First, evaluation should always include cross-dataset or unseen-generator tests in addition to in-domain accuracy. Second, compression, common codecs, resizing, and realistic post-processing should be part of robustness testing. Third, adversarial evaluation should be treated as a standard security requirement for high-impact applications. Fourth, reported performance should preserve dataset, metric, preprocessing, and attack context so that results can be compared transparently. In high-stakes use, detector output should therefore support a wider verification process rather than act as the sole basis for an authenticity decision.

The reliability of this literature study is strengthened by explicit selection rules, a stable extraction structure, and triangulation across benchmark papers, primary empirical studies, a systematic review, and a recent cross-modality empirical survey. Nevertheless, the review is not exhaustive. Ten publications were purposively selected for direct relevance, which creates a risk of missing contradictory evidence. The visual literature also concentrates heavily on face manipulation, while deepfake images can now be generated without face swapping. Metrics differ across modalities: visual studies frequently report accuracy or AUC, while audio studies often use EER and task-specific cost functions. This heterogeneity makes a pooled meta-analysis inappropriate.

Validity is further limited by the operational definition of real-world conditions. Internet-sourced datasets, cross-domain tests, codecs, and adversarial attacks approximate deployment challenges, but no single benchmark reproduces all real-world situations. New generators can also appear faster than peer-reviewed evaluation cycles. The results therefore justify a bounded conclusion about current passive detection rather than a permanent judgement about future systems.

In terms of usability, the findings are directly useful for selecting and evaluating deepfake detectors: benchmark accuracy alone is insufficient, and generalisation and robustness tests are essential. The evidence does not justify a universal threshold or a single 'best' detector across image, video, and audio. Future work should prioritise diverse and continuously updated datasets, standardised cross-domain protocols, robust feature learning, and evaluation against realistic adaptive attacks. On the evidence reviewed here, AI-based deepfake detection is an effective but fallible layer of defence: strong under known conditions, materially less dependable when those conditions change.
