## Abstract

AI-based deepfake detectors can achieve very high accuracy on controlled benchmarks, but this does not automatically mean that they are reliable when confronted with new generators, internet-sourced media, compression, or deliberate evasion. This study investigates the reliability of AI-based deepfake detection across image, video, and audio through a structured literature study of twelve selected scholarly publications from 2019-2026. The evidence was analysed in three stages: detection-technique taxonomy, contextual comparison of reported performance, and synthesis of factors that reduce effectiveness. The literature shows that modern detectors use spatial, frequency, temporal, fingerprint, and learned representation approaches for visual media, while audio systems increasingly learn spectro-temporal representations directly from speech. Within-domain results can be near-perfect, yet cross-domain evaluation consistently exposes a generalisation gap. A 2026 cross-modality evaluation reports double-digit performance degradation outside the training distribution and white-box adversarial attack success rates above 80% against undefended models. Compression, unseen manipulation methods, dataset shift, acoustic variation, and adversarial optimisation further reduce reliability. Complementary invisible watermarking can provide explicit provenance signals when a participating generator embeds them, but watermark-removal research shows that such signals are not inherently tamper-proof. The study concludes that current detectors are conditionally reliable: effective as screening tools under known conditions, but insufficiently robust to be treated as universally dependable evidence of authenticity.

**Keywords:** deepfake detection; generalisation; robustness; adversarial attacks; image forensics; audio anti-spoofing; watermarking; provenance

## 1. Introduction

Deepfakes are synthetic or manipulated images, videos, and audio recordings created with generative models in ways that can make false content appear authentic. Their practical relevance has increased as generation systems have become easier to use and capable of producing increasingly convincing media. The consequences are not limited to entertainment or harmless editing: deepfakes can support impersonation, fraud, misinformation, reputational harm, and the erosion of trust in digital evidence. Recent cross-modality research therefore treats reliable detection as a security and information-integrity problem rather than only a computer-vision benchmark task (Nguyen-Le et al., 2026).

A large research community has developed detectors that look for visual or acoustic traces left by synthesis and manipulation. Controlled benchmarks demonstrate that these systems can perform extremely well. FaceForensics++, for example, established one of the most influential visual benchmarks with more than 1.8 million manipulated images and showed that data-driven detectors can remain strong even when compression is present (Rössler et al., 2019). For audio, AASIST demonstrated that a single graph-attention architecture can detect spoofing artefacts across spectral and temporal intervals, achieving an equal error rate (EER) of 0.83% on the ASVspoof 2019 Logical Access benchmark (Jung et al., 2022). Such results can suggest that the detection problem is close to being solved.

However, benchmark accuracy is only one dimension of reliability. A detector may learn properties that are specific to the dataset, compression pipeline, manipulation method, or generator used during training. When those conditions change, performance can decline sharply. WildDeepfake was created precisely because datasets recorded with a limited number of actors and generated with a small set of tools did not represent the diversity of deepfakes circulating online; its internet-collected material proved substantially more challenging for existing detectors (Zi et al., 2020). More recent work similarly identifies generalisation and robustness, rather than in-domain accuracy alone, as the main barriers to dependable deployment (Nguyen-Le et al., 2026; Ramanaharan et al., 2025).

The objective of this paper is therefore to determine to what extent current AI-based deepfake detection can be considered reliable when evidence from images, videos, and audio is considered together. Reliability is operationalised as a combination of discriminatory performance, generalisation to data or generators outside the training distribution, and robustness to transformations or attacks. Real-world conditions are operationalised as conditions that differ meaningfully from a detector's controlled training benchmark, including internet-sourced media, cross-dataset evaluation, unseen manipulation methods, compression or codecs, diverse recording conditions, and deliberate adversarial evasion. The primary scope is passive AI/ML-based detection of image, video, and audio content. In response to lecturer feedback, the paper also includes a bounded analysis of invisible watermarking as an active provenance mechanism for AI-generated images. Legal analysis and the development of a new detector or watermarking method remain outside the scope.

The main research question is: To what extent can AI-based deepfake detection systems reliably distinguish AI-generated or manipulated media from authentic content? Three subquestions support the analysis: (1) Which AI and machine learning techniques are currently used to detect deepfake images, videos, and audio? (2) How accurately can current deepfake detection systems identify manipulated content under real-world conditions? (3) What factors, such as video compression, new generative models, or deliberate attempts to avoid detection, reduce the effectiveness of deepfake detection systems? The paper first explains the literature-study method, then presents results for the three subquestions, and finally integrates these findings into a conclusion and discussion.

## 2. Method

A structured literature study with document analysis was used. The method was deliberately narrower than a full systematic literature review because the assignment timeframe is four weeks and the research question requires a focused synthesis rather than exhaustive coverage of every deepfake detector. The candidate population consisted of scholarly publications on passive AI-based deepfake detection for image, video, or audio, supplemented by literature on invisible watermarking for generative images because the lecturer explicitly requested this aspect. The final sample contained twelve publications selected to provide direct evidence for detector techniques, measured performance, generalisation, robustness, realistic evaluation conditions, and the capabilities and limitations of watermark-based provenance.

The source search focused on peer-reviewed conference and journal literature available through publisher and conference repositories including SpringerLink, IEEE/CVF Open Access, NeurIPS Proceedings, ISCA Archive, and ScienceDirect. Searches and source verification were conducted in September 2026. Search terms combined deepfake detection with concepts such as benchmark, generalization/generalisation, cross-dataset, real-world, compression, adversarial attack, audio anti-spoofing, robustness, invisible watermarking, provenance, and watermark removal. Publisher or conference versions were treated as the primary records; arXiv copies were consulted only when they mirrored an included peer-reviewed publication. The main evidence window was 2020-2026, with FaceForensics++ (2019) retained as a foundational benchmark because it directly addresses compression and remains widely used by later studies.

Publications were included when they directly supported at least one research question or the lecturer-requested watermarking analysis and contained a clear evidence basis. Performance studies had to preserve sufficient context to interpret their result, such as dataset, modality, evaluation protocol, or attack condition. Generation-only studies, unsupported commentary, duplicate versions, and papers outside the defined modalities were excluded from the final sample. The sample contains primary empirical studies, benchmark papers, one systematic review, one recent cross-modality survey that also performs its own empirical evaluation, and two peer-reviewed watermarking studies selected to represent both capability and removal risk. This combination allows broad field mapping while keeping major reliability claims connected to measured evidence.

A structured extraction matrix was used as the research instrument. For each paper, the analysis recorded publication year, modality, detector or technique family, datasets, evaluation setting, performance metric where relevant, cross-domain or unseen-generator conditions, compression or distortion conditions, adversarial conditions, and stated limitations. Results were then analysed per subquestion. For SQ1, techniques were grouped into a bounded taxonomy. For SQ2, performance was compared contextually rather than pooled across incompatible metrics. For SQ3, evidence was grouped into degradation and failure factors. The main research question was answered by integrating these three analyses and weighting highly generalisable evidence more strongly than isolated in-domain accuracy.

Methodological reliability was supported by stable source selection criteria, explicit operational definitions, traceable extraction fields, and the use of multiple independent studies for important claims. Validity was protected by separating within-domain performance from cross-domain generalisation and by avoiding direct numerical comparison between incompatible visual and audio metrics. The method remains limited by the small purposive sample, the rapid evolution of generative models, and the dominance of face-manipulation research within the visual literature. These limitations are considered explicitly in the discussion.
