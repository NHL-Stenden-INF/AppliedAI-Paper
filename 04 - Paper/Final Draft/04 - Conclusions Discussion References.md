## 4. Conclusions and Discussion

The main research question asked to what extent AI-based deepfake detection systems can reliably distinguish manipulated or AI-generated media from authentic content. The evidence supports a conditional answer. Current systems can achieve very strong measured performance within the distributions and attack types represented by their training and benchmark data, but that performance does not generalise consistently to real-world conditions. Across image, video, and audio, the largest weakness is not the inability to fit known datasets; it is the loss of performance when the generation method, dataset, compression pipeline, acoustic environment, or attacker behaviour changes.

SQ1 showed that current detection techniques span forensic cues, learned spatial and frequency representations, temporal modelling, generator fingerprints, hybrid models, and audio systems that learn spectro-temporal artefacts. The lecturer-requested watermarking analysis adds an active provenance approach: participating generators can embed a signal that can later support attribution, but this should be interpreted separately from passive detection. Generalisation-oriented techniques such as SBI and UCF deliberately try to learn common forgery signals rather than manipulation-specific shortcuts. SQ2 showed that controlled benchmarks can produce near-perfect or very low-error results, but cross-domain testing introduces a persistent double-digit performance gap and internet/crowdsourced data are substantially more challenging. SQ3 showed that compression, unseen generators, adversarial attacks, watermark absence or removal, dataset shift, and inconsistent evaluation protocols all limit reliability.

The practical implication is that a detector score should be interpreted as evidence conditioned on an evaluation context, not as a universal probability that content is fake. A model validated only on one benchmark should not be assumed reliable for media from an unknown generator or distribution. For deployment, the findings support five recommendations. First, evaluation should always include cross-dataset or unseen-generator tests in addition to in-domain accuracy. Second, compression, common codecs, resizing, and realistic post-processing should be part of robustness testing. Third, adversarial evaluation should be treated as a standard security requirement for high-impact applications. Fourth, reported performance should preserve dataset, metric, preprocessing, and attack context so that results can be compared transparently. Fifth, where generation systems support robust watermarking, a verified mark can be used as additional provenance evidence, but the absence of a mark should never be treated as proof of authenticity because unwatermarked generators and removal attacks remain possible (Fernandez et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024). In high-stakes use, passive detector output and watermark verification should therefore support a wider verification process rather than act as the sole basis for an authenticity decision.

The reliability of this literature study is strengthened by explicit selection rules, a stable extraction structure, and triangulation across benchmark papers, primary empirical studies, a systematic review, and a recent cross-modality empirical survey. Nevertheless, the review is not exhaustive. Twelve publications were purposively selected for direct relevance, which creates a risk of missing contradictory evidence. The visual literature also concentrates heavily on face manipulation, while deepfake images can now be generated without face swapping. Metrics differ across modalities: visual studies frequently report accuracy or AUC, while audio studies often use EER and task-specific cost functions. This heterogeneity makes a pooled meta-analysis inappropriate.

Validity is further limited by the operational definition of real-world conditions. Internet-sourced datasets, cross-domain tests, codecs, and adversarial attacks approximate deployment challenges, but no single benchmark reproduces all real-world situations. New generators can also appear faster than peer-reviewed evaluation cycles. The results therefore justify a bounded conclusion about current passive detection, with watermarking treated as complementary image-provenance evidence rather than as a permanent judgement about future detectors, generators, or provenance systems.

In terms of usability, the findings are directly useful for selecting and evaluating deepfake detectors: benchmark accuracy alone is insufficient, and generalisation and robustness tests are essential. The evidence does not justify a universal threshold or a single 'best' detector across image, video, and audio. Future work should prioritise diverse and continuously updated datasets, standardised cross-domain protocols, robust feature learning, evaluation against realistic adaptive attacks, and watermarking schemes whose provenance guarantees and removal resistance are evaluated under clearly stated threat models. On the evidence reviewed here, AI-based deepfake detection is an effective but fallible layer of defence: strong under known conditions, materially less dependable when those conditions change.
